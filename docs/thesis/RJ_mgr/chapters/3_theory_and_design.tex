\chapter{Theoretical background and system design}\label{sec:theory}

This chapter establishes the theory that motivates the design of the system and then describes the system itself. Sections~\ref{sec:forecast_combination} to~\ref{sec:hypotheses} introduce forecast-combination theory, map it onto the trading problem, and state the three hypotheses that the experiments test. Sections~\ref{sec:system} to~\ref{subsec:taxonomy} describe the architecture, the five signal agents, the decision agent that aggregates them, and the taxonomy that makes the heterogeneity of the agent pool explicit.

Throughout the thesis, the term \emph{ensemble} denotes the complete five-agent system together with its decision agent, as opposed to the single-agent and leave-one-agent-out variants used as baselines in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

\section{Forecast combination and variance reduction}\label{sec:forecast_combination}

Forecast-combination theory supplies the reasoning behind the decision agent. Consider $K$ forecasts $f_1, \ldots, f_K$ of a target $y$ that is not observable at the time of forecasting. Write $e_k = y - f_k$ for the error of the $k$th forecast, $\sigma_k^2$ for the variance of that error and $\rho_{kj}$ for the correlation between the errors of forecasts $k$ and $j$. Any convex combination, meaning any set of weights $w_k \geq 0$ summing to one, then carries a combined error whose variance is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}\!\left( y - \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k f_k \right)
= \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{j=1}^{K} w_k w_j \rho_{kj} \sigma_k \sigma_j
\label{eq:combined_variance}
\end{equation}
and this is generally smaller than the variance of the individual forecasts entering it, provided they are not perfectly correlated \cite{batesgranger1969,clemen1989,timmermann2006}. Empirical work over several decades has found that even the simplest combinations, equal-weight averages among them, tend to beat their individual members across a wide range of settings \cite{clemen1989,timmermann2006}.

The two-forecaster case repays working through, because it exhibits every property the later design relies on. Setting $K = 2$ and placing weight $w$ on $f_1$ gives a combined error $e_c = w e_1 + (1-w) e_2$, at which \eqref{eq:combined_variance} reduces to
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(e_c) = w^2 \sigma_1^2 + (1-w)^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2w(1-w)\,\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 .
\label{eq:two_forecaster}
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to $w$ and equating the derivative to zero yields the variance-minimising weight
\begin{equation}
w^\star = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2},
\label{eq:optimal_weight}
\end{equation}
which is the classical result of \cite{batesgranger1969}. Substituting $w^\star$ back into \eqref{eq:two_forecaster} gives the smallest variance the pair can attain,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(e_c^\star) = \frac{\sigma_1^2 \sigma_2^2 \left(1 - \rho^2\right)}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}.
\label{eq:min_variance}
\end{equation}

Three readings of \eqref{eq:two_forecaster} to \eqref{eq:min_variance} shape everything that follows. Putting $\rho = 0$ into \eqref{eq:optimal_weight} leaves $w^\star = \sigma_2^2/(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$, so that under uncorrelated errors the optimal weights are proportional to inverse variances, $w_k \propto 1/\sigma_k^2$. That is the precision-weighting intuition Section~\ref{sec:mapping} borrows and Section~\ref{subsec:confvalid} eventually puts to the test. Putting $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = \sigma$ and $w = 1/2$ instead reduces \eqref{eq:two_forecaster} to $\sigma^2 (1 + \rho)/2$, which sits below $\sigma^2$ for every $\rho < 1$ and keeps falling as $\rho$ does; equally accurate forecasters need only to disagree, not to be individually strong. And since $\partial \mathrm{Var}(e_c) / \partial \rho = 2w(1-w)\sigma_1\sigma_2 > 0$ at any interior weight, the combined variance rises monotonically with correlation, so a negatively correlated pair does strictly better than an uncorrelated one. Deliberately opposed agents are therefore an asset to be sought rather than an awkwardness to be tolerated.

Two consequences of the general form \eqref{eq:combined_variance} bear directly on the design of a multi-agent trading system. The first has just been derived in miniature: because the combined variance rises with every $\rho_{kj}$, agents that disagree are worth more than agents that agree, and agents that disagree systematically are worth most of all. That is the argument for populating the pool with methodologically complementary members rather than with variations on a single idea, and it predicts, for instance, that a momentum-family agent and a mean-reversion-family agent will produce negatively correlated signals whenever the market is trendless.

The second is that the mechanism degrades gracefully. Nothing in \eqref{eq:combined_variance} privileges any particular member of the pool, so the variance reduction persists for as long as two imperfectly correlated forecasters remain within it. Section~\ref{subsec:ablation} turns that prediction into the leave-one-agent-out ablation.

\section{Mapping to the trading problem}\label{sec:mapping}

Carrying that framework over to trading requires one identification. On date $t$ and for symbol $s$, every signal agent $a$ emits a direction $\sigma_{a,s}(t) \in \{-1, 0, +1\}$ paired with a confidence $c_{a,s}(t) \in [0, 1]$, and it is the confidence that is made to stand in for the inverse variance appearing in \eqref{eq:optimal_weight}. The stand-in is heuristic, monotone and bounded: a larger value means the indicator of the agent has moved further inside its active region, which the design reads as a sign that the agent is on firmer ground at that moment.

Those per-agent readings are aggregated by the decision agent into a single score
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{score}(t, s) \;=\; \sum_{a} w_{\kappa(a)} \, c_{a,s}(t) \, \sigma_{a,s}(t),
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
in which $\kappa(a) \in \{\text{trend}, \text{momentum}, \text{mean-reversion}, \text{volatility}\}$ names the methodological family that agent $a$ belongs to, and $w_{\kappa(a)}$ is a weight attached to that family, serving as a prior on how far its members are to be trusted relative to the others. Section~\ref{subsec:decision} fixes both those weights and the thresholds that turn the score, symbol by symbol, into a buy, a sell or a hold.

The standing of this identification is limited in two ways that should be carried through the rest of the thesis. The first is that signals and confidences alike are heuristic transformations of price history rather than calibrated statistical forecasts. Predictive distributions are estimated nowhere in the system, and no confidence value is fitted to data of any kind. What connects the design to classical forecast-combination theory is accordingly an analogy and not a derivation: \eqref{eq:combined_variance} argues for an architecture of methodologically heterogeneous agents whose errors are imperfectly correlated, and it argues for giving the score \eqref{eq:score} its linear form, but nothing in it establishes that $c_{a,s}(t)$ behaves like an estimate of $1/\sigma_a^2$.

The second is that the analogy is testable, and that it is duly tested. Whether a higher confidence really does mark a more accurate signal is settled in Section~\ref{subsec:confvalid}, which relates confidence values to realised directional accuracy over the training window. The hypotheses stated next are framed so as not to rest on the answer: they concern the risk-adjusted behaviour of the pooled ensemble, which \eqref{eq:combined_variance} predicts from error diversity by itself, with no appeal to precision weighting.

\section{Hypotheses}\label{sec:hypotheses}

Three testable hypotheses follow from that mapping. The first casts the ensemble as a \emph{risk-aware} decision support tool, in keeping with what forecast-combination theory actually predicts, which is a reduction in variance rather than any systematic lift in the mean.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{H1.} Measured on held-out data with transaction costs deducted, the multi-agent system matches or betters buy-and-hold WIG20 in three respects: across the risk-adjusted measures (Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar, Ulcer, Martin), in maximum drawdown, and in a comparison of equity curves matched on volatility. This holds even should the system fail to dominate on gross total return.
\item \textbf{H2.} Measured on held-out data with transaction costs deducted, the multi-agent system beats the strongest single-agent baseline in total return. The reasoning is that a combined score spreads itself over agents whose errors differ, and should therefore surpass any one of them, barring the degenerate case where a single agent is uniformly the best of the pool.
\item \textbf{H3.} The performance of the ensemble withstands the removal of any single signal agent. The reasoning is that no one agent carries the directional signal by itself, and that variance reduction survives, by \eqref{eq:combined_variance}, for as long as two imperfectly correlated forecasters are left in the pool.
\end{itemize}

WIG20 being the primary panel, H1 is posed against buy-and-hold on that index, while Section~\ref{subsec:ftse} carries the identical battery of risk-adjusted comparisons over to the FTSE~100 by way of supplementary external evidence. H2 and H3 are tested on WIG20 and nowhere else. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} presents the evidence bearing on each and weighs how far it agrees with what the theory predicts.

\section{System architecture}\label{sec:system}

Four layers make up the system, as Fig.~\ref{fig:sys_architecture} shows. At the bottom the \emph{data layer} reads daily closing prices for the WIG20 constituents into one common panel format, disposing of missing values by dropping whichever rows are incomplete. Above it the \emph{signal layer} holds the five independent signal agents, each of which receives the history of one symbol, truncated at the current date inclusive, and returns a triple: a direction drawn from $\{-1, 0, +1\}$, a confidence drawn from $[0, 1]$, and the name of a methodological family, whether trend, momentum, mean-reversion or volatility.

The \emph{decision layer} above that consists of one decision agent, which collects every per-symbol signal issued on a given date and returns a single recommendation per symbol, either buy, sell or hold. The \emph{presentation layer} at the top holds two components. A portfolio simulator executes the recommendations at the daily closing price, keeps cash and positions after commissions and slippage, and records the resulting equity curve. A metrics calculator then derives from that curve the risk-adjusted metrics reported in Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}.

\section{Signal agents}\label{sec:agents}

Every signal agent plays the part of an independent and imperfect forecaster of the next directional move in a single symbol. Confining each one to a single methodological family is what makes the forecast-combination argument of Section~\ref{sec:forecast_combination} well defined, and it is also what drives the forecast errors of any two agents as far apart as the indicator set permits.

The indicators themselves are not contributions of this thesis, and the boundary between what is borrowed and what is original deserves to be drawn explicitly. Three of the five come from identifiable authors, and this work adopts not only their definitions but the conventional parameter settings that accompany them, which is what licenses the claim, made here and repeated in Section~\ref{subsec:walkforward}, that no indicator hyperparameter is ever tuned. Provenance is accordingly stated agent by agent below. What \emph{is} original to this work is the layer constructed on top of the indicators: the confidence function attached to each one, the assignment of agents to methodological families, and the family weights of \eqref{eq:family_weights}. None of the three has a source in the technical-analysis literature, and each is presented here as a design decision of the author rather than as received practice.

Each entry below gives the computation the agent performs, the source of the indicator it computes, and the forecasting role it fills, meaning the market behaviour it is built to detect together with the circumstances under which it tends to be wrong. Two conventions hold throughout: an agent returns a neutral hold signal whenever the available history is too short for its indicator, and confidence is zero everywhere outside the active signal band.

\textbf{MACD (moving-average convergence-divergence).} The indicator is due to Appel \cite{appel2005}. Subtracting a slow exponential moving average (EMA) of the closing price from a fast one, at default spans of $17$ and $8$ respectively, produces the MACD line, and taking an EMA of that line in turn, at a default span of $9$, produces the signal line. An upward crossing of the two lines makes the agent emit $+1$, a downward crossing $-1$. The confidence attached,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{confidence} = \tanh\!\left(\frac{|h|}{2\sigma}\right),
\label{eq:macd_confidence}
\end{equation}
is the author's own and has no counterpart in \cite{appel2005}; here $h$ is the current histogram value and $\sigma$ its standard deviation over the preceding $50$ bars, so that the hyperbolic tangent carries a normalised histogram magnitude into $[0, 1)$. Role: a momentum forecaster reporting how far recent directional moves have persisted, whose errors gather at trend reversals, where a crossover necessarily lags the turning point. Methodological family: momentum.

\textbf{RSI (relative strength index).} The index was introduced by Wilder \cite{wilder1978}. It compares the average gain with the average loss over a rolling window and is defined as $\mathrm{RSI} = 100 - 100/(1 + \mathrm{RS})$, where $\mathrm{RS} = \mathrm{avg\_gain}/\mathrm{avg\_loss}$. The action rule is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{action} =
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{buy}, & \mathrm{RSI} < 30, \\
\mathrm{sell}, & \mathrm{RSI} > 70, \\
\mathrm{hold}, & \mathrm{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:rsi_rule}
\end{equation}
in which the window of $14$ days and the thresholds of $30$ and $70$ are Wilder's own settings, taken over here unaltered. Confidence is the author's addition and equals the normalised distance of the index from whichever threshold lies nearer. Role: a mean-reversion forecaster betting against recent price extremes, whose errors are largest in strong trends, when the index can sit overbought or oversold for extended stretches. Methodological family: mean-reversion.

\textbf{ROC (rate of change).} Unlike the two preceding indicators the rate of change has no single originator, being standard material in the reference literature on technical indicators \cite{achelis2000,murphy1999}. It is simply the percentage change in the closing price across a fixed number of days, here $10$. The action rule is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{action} =
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{buy}, & \mathrm{ROC} > \tau, \\
\mathrm{sell}, & \mathrm{ROC} < -\tau, \\
\mathrm{hold}, & \mathrm{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:roc_rule}
\end{equation}
where the threshold $\tau$, set by default to $0.02$, and the confidence, taken as $|\mathrm{ROC}|$ clipped to $[0, 1]$, are both choices of the author rather than conventions of the literature. Role: a momentum forecaster working on a shorter horizon than MACD and responding to the bare speed of price change, whose errors arise wherever a fast move reverses without warning. Methodological family: momentum.

\textbf{Bollinger Bands.} The construction is Bollinger's \cite{bollinger2001}. A simple moving average (SMA) of the closing price over a window supplies the middle band, and the outer bands are placed at $U = \mathrm{middle} + m\sigma$ and $L = \mathrm{middle} - m\sigma$. The action rule is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{action} =
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{buy}, & \mathrm{close} < L, \\
\mathrm{sell}, & \mathrm{close} > U, \\
\mathrm{hold}, & \mathrm{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:bollinger_rule}
\end{equation}
The window of $20$ days and the multiplier $m = 2$ employed here are Bollinger's own specification. Confidence, again supplied by the author, is the normalised distance of the close from the nearer band. Role: a volatility forecaster gauging, in units of the standard deviation, how far price has pulled away from its mean, and calling a reversion once the close pierces a band; its errors occur where a breakout through a band, rather than reverting, matures into a sustained move. Since the bands respond to dispersion and not to direction, this agent carries less directional information than any of the other four, which its family weight duly reflects (Section~\ref{subsec:decision}). Methodological family: volatility.

\textbf{MA trend.} Two simple moving averages are compared, a fast one over $50$ days and a slow one over $200$. The pairing is long-standing practice in the technical-analysis literature \cite{murphy1999}, and the predictive content of moving-average crossover rules on equity data is the subject of \cite{brock1992}. A buy signal is emitted when the fast SMA exceeds the slow SMA, and a sell signal in the opposite case. Confidence, set by the author, equals the absolute difference between the two averages divided by the latest close. Role: a long-horizon trend forecaster reading the prevailing market regime off the relation between a short and a long average, whose errors accumulate in choppy, trendless markets where the two averages cross one another repeatedly. Methodological family: trend.

\section{Decision agent}\label{subsec:decision}

Signals produced on a given date arrive at the decision agent as a list, which it groups by symbol. Any signal whose confidence falls below a global minimum $c_{\min}$ is discarded outright, and whatever survives is aggregated into the score defined in \eqref{eq:score}. The family weights $w_\kappa$ entering that score are held fixed at
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
w_\mathrm{trend} &= 1.3, & w_\mathrm{momentum} &= 1.0, \\
w_\mathrm{mean\text{-}rev.} &= 0.8, & w_\mathrm{volatility} &= 0.5.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:family_weights}
\end{equation}

Encoded in these four numbers is a fixed prior on how far each methodological family may be trusted to call direction, and the ordering derives from the empirical literature rather than from any in-sample search. The largest weight goes to trend-following, because time-series momentum stands among the best-documented return predictors there are, robust and persistent alike across a range of equity markets and holding horizons \cite{moskowitz2012,asness2013}, and because daily index constituents of the WIG20 sort tend to exhibit sustained directional regimes. Shorter-horizon momentum takes a unit weight and serves as the reference family. Mean reversion sits below momentum on the grounds that overbought and oversold rules inform mainly in range-bound phases and are frequently wrong through strong trends. Volatility takes the smallest weight of the four, since band-based signals answer to dispersion rather than to direction and so carry the least directional information.

The four levels are deliberately coarse, equally spaced multiples chosen to keep the weights interpretable and the degrees of freedom low. They stay fixed across every symbol, every regime and every evaluation window, and they are never tuned on test data.

Declining to estimate the weights at all may look like a concession, but the forecast-combination literature suggests it is closer to a precaution. Section~\ref{sec:forecast_combination} derived the variance-minimising weight \eqref{eq:optimal_weight} on the assumption that $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$ and $\rho$ are known quantities. In application they are not. Each must itself be estimated from a finite sample, which makes the resulting $\hat{w}$ a random variable whose sampling error feeds straight into the variance of the combination. Where the pooled forecasters are of comparable accuracy the surface \eqref{eq:two_forecaster} is nearly flat about its optimum, so the theoretical gain from departing from equal weights is small while the estimation error incurred in hunting for that optimum is not. The two effects routinely cancel, and the second frequently dominates, with the consequence that simple fixed weights prove obstinately difficult to beat with estimated ones. The phenomenon is established enough to have earned a name, the \emph{forecast combination puzzle} \cite{smithwallis2009,claeskens2016}, and it is why a fixed prior is preferred here to a fitted weight vector.

How far the results depend on that scheme is settled empirically in Section~\ref{subsec:weights}, which perturbs each family weight by up to $\pm 50\%$ and then replaces the vector wholesale with equal, inverted, in-sample-optimised and inverse-variance-estimated alternatives. The limiting case, removing an agent altogether, is covered by the leave-one-agent-out ablation of Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}, and regime-conditional weighting is taken up as future work in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

Comparing the score against mode-dependent thresholds then yields the final decision:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{action} =
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{BUY}, & \mathrm{score} \geq \theta_\text{buy}, \\
\mathrm{SELL}, & \mathrm{score} \leq \theta_\text{sell}, \\
\mathrm{HOLD}, & \mathrm{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:decision_rule}
\end{equation}
Those thresholds are $\theta_\text{buy} = -\theta_\text{sell} = 1.2$ under \emph{passive} mode, $0.7$ under \emph{balanced} mode and $0.3$ under \emph{aggressive} mode. Passive mode imposes one further condition, namely that the sign of the score agree with the dominant trend direction, itself defined as the sign-weighted sum of the confidences of the trend-family agents.

The decision agent is governed by two parameters: the mode $m$, which takes one of the values \emph{passive}, \emph{balanced} or \emph{aggressive}, and the minimum confidence threshold $c_{\min}$. These are the only quantities anywhere in the system that walk-forward evaluation is permitted to tune. Every indicator hyperparameter remains at the literature-conventional default recorded in Section~\ref{sec:agents} throughout.

\section{Agent taxonomy}\label{subsec:taxonomy}

Three dimensions summarise the five signal agents in Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}: the methodological family each belongs to, the type of signal it produces, whether by crossover or by threshold, and the horizon of the indicator beneath it. Section~\ref{sec:agents} supplies the forecasting rationale of each family alongside the agent descriptions.

Setting the agents out this way makes the heterogeneity of the pool explicit and motivates the family-level weights $w_\kappa$, since agents expected to make the most distinct forecast errors are pooled across families carrying different weights, exactly as the variance-reduction argument of Section~\ref{sec:forecast_combination} recommends. The measured correlations bear the taxonomy out. Section~\ref{subsec:correlations} locates the largest absolute correlations in cross-family pairs, RSI against ROC and Bollinger against ROC among them, and finds them carrying opposing signs, which is what two rules reading the same raw price data in opposite ways ought to produce.

\section{Chapter summary}\label{sec:design_summary}

Forecast-combination theory predicts that pooling imperfectly correlated forecasters reduces the variance of the combined forecast, and that the reduction is largest when the pooled forecasters make errors of different kinds. The system described in this chapter is built to instantiate that prediction: five agents, each confined to a single methodological family, feed a decision agent that combines their signals into the linear, confidence-weighted score of \eqref{eq:score} and thresholds it into a trading action.

Two design choices deserve emphasis, because the evaluation returns to both. The family weights are a fixed prior taken from the empirical literature rather than an estimated quantity, which trades potential in-sample fit for robustness. The confidence values are heuristics rather than calibrated probabilities, which means the inverse-variance reading of the score is an analogy to be tested, not an established property. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} tests both, along with the three hypotheses stated in Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}.
